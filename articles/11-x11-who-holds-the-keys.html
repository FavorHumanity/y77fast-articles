Why the question matters

A Digital ID signing key can help establish whether a credential is authentic. If a key is stolen, copied, misused or controlled without adequate oversight, trust in the system can be damaged. The critical governance question is: Who holds the keys?

I. Separate the powers

The issuer, holder and verifier have different responsibilities. No single administrator, contractor or vendor should control identity data, signing keys, approvals and audit records.

  • Apply least privilege and role separation.
  • Require more than one authorized approval for high-risk key actions.
  • Independently review key use, emergency access and audit records.
  • Define rotation, revocation and recovery responsibilities before deployment.

II. Keep sovereign authority accountable

Private signing keys should never be published in source code or stored in a public repository. Technology providers may support a system, but the design should not silently transfer sovereign decision-making authority.

  • Who authorizes key creation, activation, rotation, revocation and recovery?
  • Where would keys and protected backups physically reside?
  • Could any foreign vendor, cloud operator or remote administrator gain privileged access?
  • Are privileged actions independently logged, reviewed and attributable?
  • What happens if a key is lost, copied, suspected or confirmed compromised?

III. Offline trust must remain verifiable

Secure Toa (SI) Offline First explores how fictional credentials could be checked during weak or unavailable connectivity. SI means Superintelligence. This is a design direction, not a claim of completed or government-approved implementation.

  • Verify that the credential comes from a trusted issuer.
  • Detect whether protected credential data was altered.
  • Check expiration and the recognized signing key.
  • Use a defined, time-bounded method for offline status information.
  • Fail safely and create an auditable record when evidence cannot be verified.

3A SMART Tech

Analysis: Identify every person, system and vendor that could create, hold, use, copy, rotate, revoke or recover a signing key.

Analyst: Review the system from the perspectives of citizens, security reviewers and Samoa’s sovereignty. Ask who could be harmed and who would be accountable.

Analytical: Test fictional altered, expired, revoked and incorrectly signed credentials. Verify that failures are detected and that audit evidence cannot be changed without detection.

Secure First controls to investigate

  • Government control and clearly documented legal authority.
  • Separation of duties and least-privilege administration.
  • Protected key generation, storage, backup, rotation, revocation and destruction.
  • Independent, tamper-evident audit records and regular review.
  • Offline limits, freshness rules and safe failure procedures.
  • Tested incident response, breach notification and service recovery.
  • Interoperability without unnecessary centralization or vendor lock-in.
  • Public communication, accessible remedies and community trust.
